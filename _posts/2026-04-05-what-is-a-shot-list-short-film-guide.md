<p>A <strong>shot list</strong> is a document that lists every planned camera setup for your film — one row per shot, organised by scene. For short film makers specifically, it is the most practical pre-production tool available: more immediately useful on set than a storyboard, and more detailed than anything in the script. This is the complete guide to what a shot list is, what information goes in each row, how it differs from the documents it gets confused with, and how to structure one that works on a real shoot day.</p>

<h2>What a Shot List Is — and What It Is Not</h2>

<p>This is worth being direct about, because the confusion between related documents is genuine and slows people down.</p>

<p>A shot list is not a <strong>storyboard</strong>. A storyboard is a visual document — a frame-by-frame comic strip showing composition and camera movement. A shot list is a table of technical information. A storyboard shows what a shot looks like; a shot list describes how to achieve it and what order to get it in.</p>

<p>A shot list is not a <strong>screenplay</strong>. Your script tells the story — sluglines, action, dialogue. Your shot list is how you plan to photograph that story. The screenplay is the source material; the shot list is your interpretation of it through a camera lens.</p>

<p>A shot list is not a <strong>call sheet</strong>. A call sheet tells your cast and crew who needs to be where and when. A shot list tells your camera team what they are shooting when they arrive. Both live in pre-production; they are entirely different documents.</p>

<p>What a shot list actually is: the bridge between your script and your shoot day. It answers the question every camera operator asks the moment they arrive on set — "What are we getting today, and in what order?"</p>

<h2>What Information a Shot List Contains</h2>

<p>A complete shot list has one row per camera setup, with the following fields:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Shot number</strong> — a reference ID your crew can call out: Shot 2.1.3 means Act 2, Scene 1, Shot 3</li>
  <li><strong>Shot size</strong> — the framing distance: Extreme Close-up, Medium Close-up, Medium Shot, Full Shot, Wide Shot, Extreme Long Shot</li>
  <li><strong>Camera angle</strong> — Eye Level, Low Angle, High Angle, Dutch Angle, Overhead, Worm's Eye, or POV</li>
  <li><strong>Movement</strong> — Static, Pan, Tilt, Dolly In or Out, Track, Handheld, Steadicam, Crane, Zoom, Arc</li>
  <li><strong>Lens</strong> — focal length: 24mm, 35mm, 50mm, 85mm, telephoto. Lens choice affects compression, depth of field, and the emotional quality of a shot</li>
  <li><strong>Subject</strong> — who or what is in frame: character names, a prop, a location detail</li>
  <li><strong>Equipment</strong> — tripod, handheld, gimbal, dolly, crane, drone, monopod</li>
  <li><strong>Sound notes</strong> — Dialogue, Ambient Only, Voice Over, SFX, Music, Silent</li>
  <li><strong>Priority</strong> — must-have or nice-to-have. On any shoot day that runs long, this column is the difference between finishing and not finishing</li>
  <li><strong>VFX note</strong> — green screen, wire removal, CGI extension, anything requiring post-production work specific to this shot</li>
  <li><strong>Director's note</strong> — blocking intentions, performance notes, the mood or emotional register you are aiming for</li>
</ul>

<p>Not every field needs to be filled for every shot. A shot list with Shot Size, Angle, Subject, and Priority completed is more useful on set than a beautifully formatted spreadsheet with twelve empty columns. Fill in what genuinely helps your crew execute the setup.</p>

<h2>Why Directors Who Have Made Ten Films Still Use One</h2>

<p>The shot list is not a beginner's safety blanket that experienced directors graduate beyond. It is a <em>communication tool</em> — and that value does not decrease with experience. When your DP, your 1st AD, and your camera operator all carry the same shot list, everyone is working from the same plan. Confusion about what to set up next, which angle comes after this one, or whether a particular shot is worth the time it takes to light disappears. That shared clarity is the point.</p>

<p>Experienced directors also treat the shot list as a plan to deviate from intelligently, not a rigid script. Walking on set and immediately seeing a better angle than what you planned is a good thing — but knowing what you originally planned is what lets you recognise that the new idea is actually better. Without the plan, you are improvising from zero. With it, you are adapting from a position of knowledge.</p>

<p>The practical reality: a shoot day without a shot list turns into a series of conversations between director, DP, and 1st AD about what to do next. Those conversations eat time. Time on a short film shoot is the scarcest resource you have. A shot list buys that time back before you arrive on set.</p>

<h2>Shot List vs Storyboard: Which One Do You Need?</h2>

<p>The straightforward answer: a shot list covers <em>logistics</em>, a storyboard covers <em>visuals</em>. For most short films, especially those driven by dialogue and performance, the shot list alone is enough.</p>

<p>Use a storyboard when you have shots that require spatial pre-planning — action sequences, VFX work, or scenes where the relationship between characters and camera must be communicated visually to crew. Use a shot list for everything. When you need both, the storyboard tells you what the frame should look like and the shot list tells you how to achieve it and in what order.</p>

<h2>How a Shot List Is Organised on Set</h2>

<p>There are two approaches to ordering shots: by <strong>scene</strong> (following the script's narrative order) or by <strong>setup</strong> (grouping shots that share a camera position, regardless of where they fall in the script).</p>

<p>For short films, scene order is almost always the right choice. Your shot list mirrors your script, your crew can follow it intuitively, and continuity is easier to manage. Setup order — shooting everything from one camera position before repositioning — is more efficient for features with complex locations, or when repositioning is expensive in time or equipment. For a one-day short film shoot, scene order with sensible setup groupings within each scene covers most needs.</p>

<h2>The Hierarchy That Works for Short Films</h2>

<p>The most useful structure for a short film shot list is four levels deep: <strong>Act → Scene → Sequence → Shot</strong>.</p>

<p>The <strong>Act</strong> is the top-level container. Many short films have one act, or no act structure at all — but having the container available keeps things organised across multiple narrative sections or locations.</p>

<p>The <strong>Scene</strong> maps directly to a slugline in your screenplay. Every INT. or EXT. heading becomes a scene. This is the level your script gives you for free, and the natural unit your crew thinks in on a shoot day.</p>

<p>The <strong>Sequence</strong> is a group of shots within a scene that share the same camera setup or dramatic beat. The arrival, the confrontation, the exit. One scene might have two or three sequences; grouping them helps you batch your lighting setups efficiently.</p>

<p>The <strong>Shot</strong> is a single camera setup — one row in the printed table your crew carries on set.</p>

<h2>A Sample Shot List Entry, in Plain English</h2>

<p>Here is what a single row looks like when written out:</p>

<p><em>Shot 1.2.3 — Scene: INT. CAFÉ - DAY. Sequence: The Breakup. Size: Medium Close-up. Angle: Eye Level. Movement: Static. Lens: 85mm. Subject: Maria. Sound: Dialogue. Priority: Must-have. Description: Maria absorbs what she has just heard — hold on her face before she speaks. DR Note: No tears yet. Just the weight of it landing.</em></p>

<p>That one row gives your DP everything they need to light it, your camera operator everything they need to set it up, and your 1st AD what they need to schedule it. A short film might have forty to eighty rows like this. Building them before you arrive on set is the work that makes the shoot day run calmly — which is the same thing as giving yourself the best chance of making the film you actually planned to make.</p>
