<p>Once your shot list is built, it needs to be in a format your crew can actually use on set. A browser tab is not that format. A printed <strong>shot list PDF</strong> your DP, 1st AD, and camera operator can each hold — with shot sizes, angles, priorities, and storyboard images visible without a laptop or internet connection — is. This post covers what a professional shot list PDF looks like, why embedded images matter, what the Shot List Generator export produces, and the pre-export checklist that ensures your document is complete before you hand it to your crew.</p>

<h2>Why Your Shot List Needs to Be Shareable as a PDF</h2>

<p>Your crew arrives on set without reliable internet. Your 1st AD is moving between departments. Your DP is setting up the first lighting rig before you arrive. Your camera operator wants to know what comes next in the schedule while they are still finishing the current setup. All of them need the shot list immediately, continuously, and without opening a browser or logging into anything.</p>

<p>A printed PDF on a clipboard solves all of that. It is the format that production has operated on for decades, and there is no better replacement for a physical document in a fast-moving shoot environment. An editable DOCX is useful for sharing with collaborators who need to annotate — your AD adding timing estimates, your producer flagging coverage concerns — but the printed PDF is what lives on set.</p>

<h2>What a Professional Shot List PDF Looks Like</h2>

<p>A well-produced shot list PDF is a structured table in landscape orientation — landscape because there are enough columns that portrait cuts content off the right edge. The table is organised by scene: a visually distinct header row identifies each scene (INT. CAFÉ - DAY, EXT. ROADSIDE - NIGHT), followed by the shot rows belonging to it.</p>

<p>Act and scene header rows use a different background colour from shot rows — typically a dark, distinct colour — so the document is scannable at a glance without reading every row. Scene and shot groupings are immediately apparent even when flicking through a printed document quickly between setups.</p>

<p>Each shot row contains all the key fields: shot number, image or storyboard thumbnail, shot size, angle, movement, lens, subject, sound, priority, VFX note, description, and director's note. The image column is where embedded storyboard thumbnails appear — if you have attached one to the shot — visible in the table without any separate file.</p>

<p>Shot numbers in the format 1.2.3 (Act 1, Scene 2, Shot 3) give every setup a clear identifier your 1st AD can call out by reference. "Moving to Shot 2.3.1" is unambiguous regardless of where you are in the shooting day. Everyone on the crew knows exactly which row to look at.</p>

<h2>Why Embedded Images Matter</h2>

<p>A shot list that references an image filename — "storyboard_shot3.jpg" — is not a useful document on set. The person reading it cannot see the image without a laptop. They would need a separate folder of files, the time to find and open the right one, and the cognitive overhead of cross-referencing them — none of which are available when you are between setups and moving fast.</p>

<p>An image embedded directly in the PDF is visible instantly, in context, alongside the shot's technical data. Your DP can look at the storyboard reference and the angle specification in the same row and begin planning the lighting setup immediately. This is the practical advantage of using a browser-based tool over a static spreadsheet template: Google Sheets can reference images but cannot embed them in a print-ready PDF. The Shot List Generator embeds every image you have attached — uploaded reference photos, location stills, and in-browser sketches — directly into the exported PDF and DOCX.</p>

<h2>How to Prepare Your Shot List Before Exporting</h2>

<p>A pre-export review takes ten minutes and prevents the worst kind of on-set problem: discovering that a shot's information is incomplete after you have already printed and handed out the document. Work through this checklist before clicking Export:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Fill in must-have fields for every shot</strong> — at minimum: shot size, angle, and subject. A row with these three complete is usable on set; a row with all fields empty is not.</li>
  <li><strong>Upload or sketch storyboard images</strong> for your priority shots — specifically the must-haves that have complex compositions, VFX requirements, or unusual spatial arrangements. Nice-to-have shots can remain without images.</li>
  <li><strong>Check every Priority field</strong> — every shot should be marked must-have or nice-to-have. If you have not done this during the build phase, do it now. It is the most useful column in a printed shot list under real shoot-day pressure.</li>
  <li><strong>Review sequence order within each scene</strong> — does the sequence order match how you actually intend to shoot? Reorder now, before printing. Drag to reorder in the tool; confirm the shot numbers look correct before exporting.</li>
  <li><strong>Save a JSON backup before exporting</strong> — the Shot List Generator lets you save your complete shot list as a JSON file and reload it in any future session. Do this before the export so you have a recoverable version if you need to revise after seeing the printed document.</li>
</ol>
