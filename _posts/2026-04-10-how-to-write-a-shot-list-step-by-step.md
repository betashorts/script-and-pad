<p>The most common reason first-time directors skip the <strong>shot list</strong> is the belief that it will slow them down. The opposite is true. A shot list does not restrict your instincts on set — it liberates them, because you arrive knowing the plan and spend your creative energy adapting it rather than inventing it from nothing under pressure. This is a complete, step-by-step guide to writing a shot list from scratch, for directors building their first one.</p>

<h2>Why First-Time Directors Skip It — and Why That Is a Mistake</h2>

<p>Skipping the shot list feels spontaneous. What it actually is: arriving on set without a plan, then spending the creative part of your brain on logistics — figuring out what to shoot next — while your cast and crew wait. That trade-off costs you time, takes, and often the specific shots you actually needed. The shot list moves the logistics thinking to prep, so your instincts on the day are free to respond to what actually happens in front of the camera.</p>

<p>A more practical reason: your crew needs to know what is coming. Your 1st AD cannot schedule a shooting day around "we'll figure it out." Your DP cannot pre-light a setup they do not know about yet. Your cast needs to know how much time they have before the next scene. The shot list makes all of those conversations possible before the shoot begins.</p>

<h2>The 7 Steps to Writing a Shot List from Scratch</h2>

<h3>Step 1: Lock Your Script</h3>

<p>Do not begin your shot list until your script is locked. Writing shots to a script that is still changing is wasted work — every rewrite potentially invalidates what you planned. Once the script is final, the shot list can begin. If you are still working on your script, the guide to <a href="/how-to-write-a-short-film-script/">writing a short film script</a> covers what locked means and when you are actually there.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Read Once for Visual Rhythm</h3>

<p>Before building any structure, read the script once from start to finish as if you are watching the finished film. Note instinctively where you feel cuts happening — where the camera wants to move closer, pull back, change angle. These instinctive moments are where your shot decisions are already forming. Mark them loosely in the margin. This pass is not about planning yet — it is about activating your visual sense of the material before the technical work begins.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Break the Script into Scenes</h3>

<p>Every slugline in your script — every INT. or EXT. heading — is a scene. List them all. This becomes the Scene level in your shot list hierarchy. If you upload your script to the Shot List Generator, this step is automatic: the tool reads every INT. and EXT. slugline and creates the scene structure for you, without any manual typing. For the full breakdown process, read <a href="/how-to-break-down-script-scenes-shots/">How to Break Down a Script for a Short Film</a>.</p>

<h3>Step 4: Within Each Scene, Identify Your Setups</h3>

<p>A <strong>setup</strong> is one camera position. Every time you move the camera to a new position, you are in a new setup. A dialogue scene between two characters typically needs three setups: a wide master shot, a close-up on Character A, and a close-up on Character B. An action scene might need seven. A monologue might need two — one wide, one close. Identifying your setups is identifying your <em>sequences</em> within each scene. Name them loosely: "The Argument," "The Escape," "The Reveal."</p>

<h3>Step 5: For Each Setup, List Every Shot Needed</h3>

<p>Within each setup (sequence), list every individual shot you plan to capture from that camera position before you move. A master shot might only need one angle. A close-up position might give you the main reaction plus a cutaway. List each as a separate row. This is the Shot level — and it is where the shot list becomes genuinely useful on the day, because each row is a specific camera instruction your crew can execute without interpretation.</p>

<h3>Step 6: For Each Shot, Fill In the Fields</h3>

<p>For each row, fill in the technical fields your crew needs:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Shot size</strong> — Extreme Close-up, Medium Close-up, Medium Shot, Full Shot, Wide Shot, Extreme Long Shot. The full guide to <a href="/camera-angles-and-shots-explained/">camera angles and shot types</a> covers every option with examples.</li>
  <li><strong>Angle</strong> — Eye Level, Low Angle, High Angle, Dutch, Overhead, Worm's Eye, POV</li>
  <li><strong>Movement</strong> — Static, Pan, Tilt, Dolly In/Out, Track, Handheld, Steadicam, Crane Up/Down, Zoom In/Out, Arc</li>
  <li><strong>Lens</strong> — focal length or descriptive term: wide (24mm), standard (35mm), normal (50mm), portrait (85mm), telephoto (135mm+)</li>
  <li><strong>Subject</strong> — character name, prop, or brief description of what is in frame</li>
  <li><strong>Sound</strong> — Dialogue, Ambient Only, SFX, Voice Over, Music, Silent</li>
  <li><strong>Priority</strong> — must-have or nice-to-have (covered in detail below)</li>
</ul>

<p>Add a Description — one to two sentences about what happens in the shot. Add a Director's Note for anything about performance, mood, or blocking that your DP or cast needs to know before arriving at this setup.</p>

<h3>Step 7: Mark Priority — Must-Have vs Nice-to-Have</h3>

<p>Every shot gets a priority designation: must-have or nice-to-have. This is the step most first-time directors skip, and it is the most important column on any shoot day that runs long — which is every shoot day. When your 1st AD tells you at 4pm that you are two hours behind schedule, you need to know instantly which shots you can cut without destroying the film. The priority column answers that question without requiring a difficult conversation under pressure, with the whole crew listening.</p>

<p>A shot is must-have if the film cannot be edited together without it. It is nice-to-have if it is a coverage bonus — a cutaway, an insert, an additional angle — that improves the film but is not structurally essential. A good rule: if you can cut the film without that shot, it is nice-to-have.</p>

<h2>How to Handle Dialogue Scenes</h2>

<p>Most short films are dialogue-heavy. The standard approach to covering a two-person scene is:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Wide master shot</strong> — the full scene from a distance, establishing the space and both characters. This is your safety net in the edit.</li>
  <li><strong>Singles</strong> — a close-up or medium close-up on each character, covering the full scene (or the relevant portion) from each position.</li>
  <li><strong>Over-the-shoulder (OTS)</strong> — slightly wider than a single, showing the back of the listening character's head with the speaking character in focus.</li>
</ul>

<p>Full coverage (master + singles + OTS for both characters) gives your editor maximum flexibility but takes significant time. Many short film makers working with a single camera choose a leaner approach: master plus singles, or even just singles — trusting close-ups to carry the scene without a wide. Decide this in advance and build it into the shot list so your 1st AD can schedule time accurately.</p>

<h2>How to Handle Action and Movement Shots</h2>

<p>Action and movement shots require more specific description, not less. For any shot involving camera movement — a dolly, a Steadicam walk, a crane move — write out the start and end position in the Description field, not just the movement type. "Dolly In" alone does not tell your camera operator where to start or how far to push. "Dolly In from Wide Shot to Medium Close-up on Sarah as she reaches the door" is executable without clarification on the day.</p>

<p>For shots involving actors moving through the frame, note the blocking reference. Your 1st AD and script supervisor need to know where each actor starts so continuity can be maintained across multiple takes and setups.</p>

<h2>The Priority Column: Why It Matters More Than Any Other</h2>

<p>On a one-day short film shoot, you will almost certainly fall behind schedule at some point. It is not a failure — it is the nature of production. What separates directors who finish their shot list from directors who do not is not talent or speed: it is knowing in advance which shots they can sacrifice.</p>

<p>The priority column is the answer. Review it before shooting each scene. Know your must-haves. Everything marked nice-to-have is a bonus you go for if time permits. Everything marked must-have is non-negotiable until it is in the camera. This discipline — built into the shot list before the shoot begins — makes you a more decisive director on the day, because the hard decisions have already been made in prep, without the pressure of a waiting crew.</p>
