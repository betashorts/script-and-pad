<p>If you have searched for a <strong>shot list template</strong> for your short film, you have probably found a Google Sheets spreadsheet or an Excel file with a row for every shot and a column for every field. These are a reasonable starting point. But static templates have a fundamental limitation that becomes apparent the moment you try to print one for your DP: they cannot embed images, they have no hierarchy, and they break the moment you try to reorder. This post covers what a good shot list template actually needs, why spreadsheet templates fall short, and how to use a free browser-based tool as a template that is already structured correctly and exports to a print-ready PDF or editable DOCX when you are done.</p>

<h2>Why Static Templates — Google Sheets, Excel, PDF Forms — Fail on Set</h2>

<p>A static spreadsheet template has three problems that compound as your shot list grows.</p>

<p>First, they <strong>cannot embed images</strong>. Your shot list's image column — where you put reference photos, location stills, or storyboard sketches — ends up as a filename in a static template: "storyboard_shot3.jpg." On set, that is useless. The crew member holding the printed sheet cannot see the image. A useful shot list has the image embedded directly in the document, in the row, next to the shot's technical data.</p>

<p>Second, they <strong>have no hierarchy</strong>. A flat spreadsheet treats every row as equal. A real shot list for even a five-minute film needs structure: Act → Scene → Sequence → Shot. Without it, when you have sixty shots across twelve scenes, finding the shots for Scene 7 requires scrolling through a wall of identical rows. Structure makes the document navigable on a shoot day when time is short and everyone needs to find their setup quickly.</p>

<p>Third, they <strong>cannot be reordered without breaking</strong>. If you decide the night before your shoot that you want to swap two scenes, you are in for a manual copy-paste session in Google Sheets. A proper tool lets you drag scenes, sequences, and shots to new positions and renumbers everything automatically.</p>

<h2>What a Good Shot List Template Actually Needs</h2>

<p>A well-designed shot list template — whether static or interactive — should include these fields as a minimum:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Shot number, ideally auto-generated from the Act/Scene/Shot hierarchy</li>
  <li>Image or storyboard reference — embedded, not a filename</li>
  <li>Shot size (ECU, MCU, MS, FS, WS, ELS)</li>
  <li>Camera angle (Eye Level, Low, High, Dutch, Overhead, POV)</li>
  <li>Camera movement (Static, Pan, Dolly, Handheld, Steadicam, etc.)</li>
  <li>Lens (focal length or descriptor)</li>
  <li>Subject or actor name</li>
  <li>Sound notes</li>
  <li>Priority (must-have / nice-to-have)</li>
  <li>Description</li>
  <li>Director's note</li>
</ul>

<p>Additionally: the ability to group shots by Scene and Sequence so the printed document is navigable without sorting columns. And the ability to export the whole structured document — with embedded images — as a landscape PDF your crew can hold and read on set.</p>

<p>If you upload your script (PDF or DOCX), the tool auto-detects every INT. and EXT. slugline and creates the Scene structure automatically — you do not need to type scene names. <h3>Step 2: Add Sequences per Camera Setup</h3>

<p>Inside each Scene, add a Sequence for each distinct camera position or dramatic beat — "The Arrival," "The Confrontation," "The Exit." Sequences are the batching unit for your lighting setups: all shots in one Sequence share the same camera position, so you finish one Sequence before repositioning and moving to the next.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Add Shots and Fill In the Fields</h3>

<p>Inside each Sequence, add individual Shots. For each shot, fill in the fields your crew needs: shot size, angle, movement, lens, subject, sound, priority, description, and a director's note. Not every field needs to be filled — start with size, angle, subject, and priority, then add lens and movement detail where it matters. The complete guide to filling in each field is in <a href="/how-to-write-a-shot-list-step-by-step/">How to Write a Shot List: Step-by-Step for First-Time Directors</a>.</p>

<p>For any shot where a visual reference matters — a complex composition, an action beat, a VFX requirement — you can upload a reference photo, a scanned sketch, or draw directly in the browser on the shot card. That image will be embedded in your PDF and DOCX export. This is what no static template can do: your storyboard reference lives inside the shot list, in the same document as the technical data, in the same row as the shot it belongs to.</p>

<h3>Step 5: Reorder as Needed, Then Export</h3>

<p>Drag shots, sequences, and scenes to reorder — the hierarchy renumbers automatically. When you are satisfied with the structure, click Export PDF or Export DOCX. The file downloads directly to your computer.</p>

<h2>The Real Advantage Over a Spreadsheet Template</h2>

<p>The critical difference is embedded images. Your storyboard references are in the document — not referenced as filenames, not in a separate folder, not requiring a laptop to view. They are in the image column, next to the shot's size, angle, and lens. Your DP can look at the reference and the technical specification in the same row. Your 1st AD can see priorities in context. Your camera operator can understand the frame without asking.</p>

<p>A Google Sheets template with a column labeled "Image" and a filename typed into it requires everyone who receives the sheet to also have the image folder, to know the file naming convention, and to cross-reference manually. On a short film with a small crew moving fast between setups, that friction compounds into lost time and missed shots.</p>
